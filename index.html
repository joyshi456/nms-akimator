THE LANGUAGE LAB

How human languages are structured — and how to engineer your own.

What Is Language?

Language is a system that maps sound to meaning through layered rules. The field that studies how it works is linguistics.

The Layers of Language

Linguists analyze language as a stack of levels, from the smallest unit to the largest:

Phonology
The inventory of speech sounds and the rules for combining them — p, a, sh.
Morphology
How sounds form meaningful pieces (morphemes) and words — cat, un-, -s.
Lexicon
The vocabulary: the full set of words a language has.
Syntax
The rules for ordering words into sentences — The cat runs.
Semantics
How sentences carry meaning and refer to the world.

Notable Facts

Roughly 7,000 languages are spoken today; about 40% are endangered.
Any healthy child can acquire any human language to native fluency.
Humans can produce ~150 distinct speech sounds; each language selects a subset.
English uses ~44 sounds to build over a million words.
Invented languages (conlangs) like Klingon, Dothraki, and Elvish have full grammars.
All human languages can express the same ideas — none is "more primitive" than another.

Sounds (Phonology)

Every word is assembled from speech sounds, called phonemes. They fall into two broad classes.

Consonants vs Vowels

Consonants

Airflow is obstructed by the lips, teeth, or tongue.

p t k m s l

Vowels

Air flows freely; only the tongue and lip shape change.

a e i o u

Relevance to Conlanging

The first design decision for any language is its phoneme inventory — which consonants and vowels it permits.

Hawaiian uses only ~13 phonemes; Taa (a Khoisan language) uses over 100, including dozens of click consonants.

Words (Morphology)

Sounds combine into syllables, and meaningful pieces called morphemes combine into words.

Syllable Structure

Using C for consonant and V for vowel, a syllable follows a pattern:

CVba, mi, to
CVCcat, dog, sun
CCVsto, gra, flu
CCVCstop, frog, spin
VCat, in, up

Japanese and Hawaiian permit mostly simple CV syllables. English tolerates extremes like CCCVCCC ("strengths").

Morphemes: The Atoms of Meaning

A morpheme is the smallest unit that carries meaning. Words are built by combining them:

cat + s = cats suffix marking plural
un + happy = unhappy prefix meaning "not"
re + play + ed = replayed "again" + past tense

Sentences (Syntax)

Syntax is the set of rules that governs how words combine into well-formed sentences. A core parameter is word order.

Word Order Across Languages

Most clauses contain a Subject, a Verb, and an Object. Languages differ in how they order them:

SOV~45%"I apple eat" — Japanese, Korean, Latin, Turkish
SVO~42%"I eat apple" — English, Spanish, Mandarin
VSO~9%"Eat I apple" — Irish, Welsh, Classical Arabic
rare~4%VOS, OVS, OSV — uncommon configurations

Types of Grammar

"Grammar" has two distinct meanings worth knowing: how languages package meaning into words (morphological typology), and how a system of rules generates valid sentences (formal grammar).

Part 1 — Morphological Typology

Languages differ in how much grammatical information they pack into a single word.

Isolating
Words rarely change shape. Grammar comes from separate words and order.
Mandarin: 我 吃 (wǒ chī, "I eat"). Past tense adds a separate particle 了 (le) rather than changing the verb.
Agglutinative
Words stack clearly separable affixes, each with one meaning.
Turkish: ev (house) → evler (houses) → evlerim (my houses) → evlerimde (in my houses).
Fusional
A single affix fuses several meanings together at once.
Spanish: hablo ("I speak"). The single ending -o encodes person, number, tense, and mood all together.
Polysynthetic
Whole sentences are packed into one very long word.
Inuktitut: tusaatsiarunnanngittualuujunga ≈ "I can't hear very well" — a single word.

Common Grammatical Features

Gender / noun class — el/la in Spanish; Swahili has more than a dozen noun classes.
Case — marking a noun's role; Latin has 6, Finnish has 15.
Tense & aspect — when an action happens and whether it's ongoing or complete.
Number — singular, plural, and sometimes dual (exactly two).

Part 2 — Formal Grammars

In linguistics and computer science, a formal grammar is a finite set of rewrite rules that can generate an infinite number of valid sentences. A rule like S → NP VP means "an S can be replaced by an NP followed by a VP."

Noam Chomsky (1956) ranked grammars by power — the Chomsky hierarchy:

Type 3 · RegularSimplest. What regular expressions match. Recognized by finite automata.
Type 2 · Context-freeHandles nesting. Describes programming-language syntax and much of natural language.
Type 1 · Context-sensitiveRules can depend on surrounding symbols. More powerful.
Type 0 · UnrestrictedAnything a computer can compute (Turing-complete).
