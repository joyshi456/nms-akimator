Why Does This Always Work? 🤔

This is the Central Limit Theorem — one of the most magical facts in all of math:

👉 If you add up many random numbers, the result is almost always shaped like a bell.

It doesn't matter what the original numbers look like — sums of randoms turn into the bell shape.

That's why the bell curve shows up everywhere in nature!

The Galton Board 🌰

A real machine that builds a bell curve from coin flips. Drop balls and watch!

How It Works ⚙️

1. A ball drops from the top.

2. It hits a peg. Bounces left or right (50/50, like a coin flip).

3. Hits another peg. Bounces left or right again.

4. After a bunch of bounces, lands in a slot at the bottom.

5. Drop tons of balls, and the slots fill up like… a bell curve! 🔔

Hash Functions: Pretend Randomness 🔐

Plot twist: a lot of "randomness" in computers isn't actually random — it's a clever trick called a hash function.

What Is a Hash? 🤔

A hash function takes any input (a word, a number, a file) and turns it into a fixed-size scrambled output that looks totally random.

But it's not random! Same input → same output, every single time. It's a deterministic recipe.

The magic: even tiny changes to the input create wildly different outputs. This is called the avalanche effect.

Hashes Look Random 🎯

Even though hashes are deterministic, when you hash lots of different things, the outputs spread out evenly — just like real randomness!

Each bar = how many hashes started with each hex digit. They're roughly equal — the hash function spreads things out evenly across all 16 buckets.

Why Should I Care? 🌐

Hash functions power tons of stuff:

🔒
Passwords
Websites store your password's hash, not the password itself.
⛓️
Bitcoin & crypto
Every block in the chain is identified by its hash.
📁
File integrity
Two files match if and only if their hashes match.
🎮
Game maps
Minecraft generates worlds from a "seed" hashed into terrain.

Mind-Benders 🤯

Three classic randomness puzzles where your gut is almost always wrong. Make a guess first, then click to reveal!

Puzzle 1 · The Birthday Paradox 🎂
In a room of 23 people, what's the chance that at least two share a birthday?

The Answer: ~50.7% 🤯

Counterintuitive, right? Here's the trick: instead of computing "at least one match," compute "no matches" and subtract from 1.

  • Person 1 picks any day: 365/365
  • Person 2 must avoid person 1's day: 364/365
  • Person 3 must avoid both: 363/365
  • … continue for all 23 people …
  • Multiply them all → ≈ 0.493 (no match)
  • So P(at least one match) = 1 − 0.493 ≈ 50.7%

This is exactly why hash collisions happen sooner than you'd think — same math.

See it for yourself 🧪

Simulate thousands of "rooms" of each size and watch the probability curve grow:

The curve crosses 50% around 23 people — and 99% by about 57. Random collisions sneak up fast!

Puzzle 2 · The Hot Streak 🔥
You flip a fair coin 10 times in a row and get all heads: HHHHHHHHHH. What's the chance the next flip is heads?

The Answer: Exactly 50% ⚖️

This is called the gambler's fallacy — believing past results affect future independent events.

Remember the Mystery Box from Tab 1? Each press is independent. The coin doesn't know it just landed heads 10 times in a row. It has no memory.

Yes, getting 10 heads in a row was unlikely before you started (about 1 in 1,024). But once you're in that situation, the next flip is just a fresh 50/50.

Prove it 🧪

Let's flip a million coins, find every "HHHHH" streak (5 heads in a row), and check what came next. Was it more likely to be tails?

After streaks of HHHHH, the next flip is heads about 50% of the time — exactly like any other flip.

Puzzle 3 · The Monty Hall Problem 🚪
You're on a game show. Behind one of three doors is a car 🚗; behind the other two, goats 🐐. You pick door #1. The host (who knows where the car is) opens door #3 to reveal a goat, then asks: "Want to switch to door #2?"

The Answer: Switch! 🚗

If you stay, you win 1/3 of the time.

If you switch, you win 2/3 of the time. Switching doubles your chances.

Why? When you first picked, you had a 1/3 chance of being right. That means 2/3 of the time, the car is behind one of the OTHER two doors. The host then helpfully eliminates the wrong one of those two — so the remaining door now carries that whole 2/3 probability.

Why Are These So Tricky? 🧠

All three puzzles share a theme: our gut is built for everyday life, not for probability.

We see "23 people" and think "small group." We see "10 heads in a row" and feel a pattern. We see "two doors left" and assume 50/50.

But math doesn't care about feelings. The only way to get good at probability is to compute it — or simulate it, just like you did above. 🎲
